I've always been interested in how much you can learn from pieces of information that seem insignificant on their own. My background is in data science and statistics, which is where that curiosity first took shape.

I didn't start out thinking about security; that came later.

It all began when I realized how much a modern browser can reveal about the person using it. That was enough to make me switch to LibreWolf, a privacy-focused fork of Firefox. From there I came across arkenfox's `user.js`, which led me into `about:config`, a corner of the browser most people never open.

I spent weeks there, learning what each setting gave away and what it cost to turn it off. Eventually it became clear that the fingerprint was only one layer: hardening the browser means little if the connection underneath betrays you.

So I kept going down the stack. First into the network, where VPNs, Tor, and I2P make very different tradeoffs. That's the part that really pulled me in, and why I now contribute to [Arti](https://gitlab.torproject.org/tpo/core/arti), Tor's Rust client ([my contributions](https://gitlab.torproject.org/moumenalaoui)). From there, I got interested in messaging, where encryption can protect what is said without necessarily hiding who is talking to whom. And then the device itself, where the operating system can undo a lot of what the layers above are trying to protect. That led me to Tails, Qubes, and GrapheneOS, which I still find the most interesting of the three.

What I took from all of this was that **no system is ever fully anonymous**. Every one of them leaks somewhere, and that is usually where things get interesting.

For most people, those tradeoffs come down to preference. For a journalist or a dissident under an authoritarian government, it can decide whether they stay alive. That's what brought me to tech policy in Washington, and to research at the Washington Institute for Near East Policy and the Special Competitive Studies Project.

The same leaks cut the other way. **What can expose a dissident can just as easily expose a regime**, which is why open-source intelligence has always pulled at me, and why I built [BLACKOUT](https://blackout.scsp.ai/), a censorship tracker hosted by SCSP. 

Tracking shutdowns raises another question: what keeps people connected when the infrastructure underneath is damaged or destroyed? That led me toward mesh networks and resilient communications, from internet shutdowns to battlefield jamming. From there it was a short step to defense, especially autonomous systems and drones, and simulating how they perform in contested environments before they're fielded.
